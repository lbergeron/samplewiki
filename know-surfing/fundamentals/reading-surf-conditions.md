# How to Read Surf Conditions Before Entering the Water

A forecast is useful, but the beach provides the final information. Spend several minutes watching before paddling out and choose conditions you can manage comfortably.

## Check the forecast

Look at several sources when possible and note:

- **Wave height:** Larger waves require stronger paddling and positioning.
- **Wave period:** A longer period often means more powerful, organized waves.
- **Wind:** Light offshore wind can groom waves; strong onshore wind can make them messy.
- **Tide:** Some breaks work only at certain tide levels.
- **Weather:** Lightning, storms, fog, and poor visibility can make surfing unsafe.

Forecast heights are estimates and do not replace observing the break.

## Observe the beach

Watch a complete set. Identify where waves break, where surfers wait, and where people exit. Look for channels, rocks, shallow areas, rip currents, and submerged obstacles.

Ask a lifeguard or local surf school about unfamiliar hazards. A lifeguarded beach is generally a better choice for beginners than an isolated break.

## Recognize wave shape

Beginner-friendly waves are usually smaller, slower, and less crowded, with a broad shoulder and a clear path to the inside. Avoid waves that close out across the beach, break directly onto shallow reef, or move faster than you can control.

The whitewater inside the main lineup is often the best place to practice paddling and standing.

## Understand currents

A rip current is a narrow flow moving away from shore. It can look like darker, calmer water, a gap in breaking waves, foam moving seaward, or a channel between sandbars.

If caught in one, stay calm, keep hold of your board, signal for help, and do not sprint directly against it. Paddle parallel to the beach until out of the strongest flow, then head shoreward.

## Make a go/no-go decision

Do not surf if:

- You cannot comfortably swim in the conditions.
- Waves are beyond your ability or equipment.
- There is lightning, dangerous debris, or no safe exit.
- The beach is too crowded for your control.
- You are tired, injured, or anxious.

Conditions can change during a session. Reassess and leave early if the ocean becomes more challenging.

## Beach checklist

- [ ] I checked the forecast and tide.
- [ ] I watched several sets from shore.
- [ ] I identified the entry, exit, and current.
- [ ] I know the local hazards and rules.
- [ ] The waves match my skill and board.
